# 1. Governing engineering distinction — binding

The Sample Conveyor photographs support a concern regarding the physical relationship between product presentation and the available narrow-belt carrying geometry, but they do not establish quantitative rated-capacity overload.

The report and future verification work must distinguish:

- **rated/design throughput capacity** — the intended mass/volumetric duty of the Sample Conveyor and its feed system; from
- **geometric carrying/containment capability** — whether the actual product-size distribution, loading quantity/distribution, usable belt width, trough geometry and belt position can physically retain the material within the carrying path.

A conveyor can be operating below its rated throughput and still experience containment loss if the lump size, loading distribution or usable carrying geometry are unsuitable. Conversely, smaller or better-distributed material may be retained at a higher throughput.

Therefore avoid using the unqualified statement **“the Sample Conveyor is overloaded”** unless later quantitative verification supports it.

Preferred engineering framing remains:

> **The observed quantity, distribution and size of the conveyed product appear capable of challenging the geometric carrying/containment capability available on the narrow Sample Conveyor belt, particularly where lateral belt offset reduces the usable carrying margin.**

# 2. Locked functional action hierarchy

The final Sample Conveyor response is diagnostic and sequential. It is not appropriate to prescribe skirting, feed restriction or product-size reduction as the sole answer before the underlying relationships are verified.

## Step 1 — Establish the intended/design basis

Confirm, where records are available:

- intended Sample Conveyor duty / design throughput;
- intended or permissible product-size range / top-size basis;
- belt width, trough/carrying arrangement and intended usable carrying geometry;
- intended loading/transfer arrangement; and
- available OEM/client operating or design requirements relevant to the Sample Conveyor system.

Where the original design basis cannot be recovered, establish an engineering verification basis from the installed system, representative operating conditions and competent/OEM review rather than inventing a limit.

## Step 2 — Validate actual feed quantity/variation and the adequacy of the existing feed-control mechanism

Establish the representative quantity of material presented to each Sample Conveyor, including normal variation and any surge/periodic high-loading condition.

Determine how the current feeder/chute arrangement controls the delivery of product to the narrow belt and whether that mechanism is adequate to regulate the required feed consistently.

The question is not only **“what is the feed rate?”** but also:

> **Is the existing feed-control arrangement capable of presenting the required quantity of product to the Sample Conveyor in a controlled and repeatable manner compatible with the belt's intended duty?**

The current photographs do not answer this quantitatively.

## Step 3 — Verify product-size distribution against the intended belt capability

Determine the representative product-size distribution, with particular attention to coarse/top-size material.

The key engineering question is:

> **Is the product-size range being presented to the Sample Conveyor compatible with the belt geometry and duty the installed conveyor was intended to handle?**

The photographs support this investigation because smaller fragments are seen remaining on the belt while larger pieces are visible outside the intended carrying path. This is evidence of a relevant size/geometry relationship, not proof of a quantified allowable top-size limit.

If the product is found to be too coarse for the installed Sample Conveyor duty, the corrective response may require upstream product-size control, feed-selection changes or another appropriate process control rather than expecting the belt/skirting alone to contain unsuitable material.

## Step 4 — Verify loading position and distribution / centring

Confirm that the feeder/chute arrangement delivers product reasonably centrally and consistently into the usable carrying area.

Feed quantity and product size can both be acceptable while containment still fails if material is loaded disproportionately toward one belt edge.

The control question is therefore:

> **Is the material being presented to the belt at the correct location and distribution for the available carrying geometry?**

This check includes the local entry covers/guides and any geometry intended to direct product from the preceding feeder/chute onto the belt.

## Step 5 — Quantify and diagnose the belt-offset / tracking condition

Document the as-found lateral belt position under representative loading before correction where practicable.

Then identify the contributing tracking/alignment/interference conditions, including as applicable:

- head/tail pulley or roller alignment;
- belt tension / loading effects;
- local guide/training arrangements;
- structural or installation geometry affecting tracking;
- product accumulation or trapped material interfering with belt freedom; and
- any other site-specific mechanical contributor established during inspection.

Sample Conveyor 2 has a captured lateral offset supported by REF-004/REF-005. Sample Conveyor 3 has a visually substantial lateral offset supported by REF-006/REF-007. Neither offset is dimensionally quantified by the still photographs.

## Step 6 — Clear/assess entry-zone congestion and correct confirmed mechanical contributors

Remove or otherwise safely address accumulated product where it prevents reliable inspection or may interfere with belt freedom/position.

Correct the confirmed alignment/tracking/interference causes rather than merely repositioning the belt temporarily.

The Sample 2 REF-006 congestion condition remains important here: accumulated coarse material may be part of the consequence, a contributor to further belt interference, or both. The photographs do not establish the chronology, so this relationship must be tested during corrective work.

## Step 7 — Re-establish the usable carrying geometry and prove centred operation under representative loading

After primary mechanical corrections, operate/observe the conveyor under representative loading and verify that the belt maintains an acceptable centred relationship to the carrying/support geometry.

The objective is not simply a centred belt while unloaded or immediately after adjustment. The belt position should remain suitable during the operating condition that matters for product containment.

## Step 8 — Determine whether feed quantity, product size or loading distribution still requires additional control

With the mechanical/tracking condition corrected, reassess whether the actual feed quantity, product-size distribution and loading position are compatible with the available carrying geometry.

Only at this point should the audit determine whether additional feed throttling/metering, product-size limitation or loading-distribution modification is required.

Different final outcomes remain possible. A tracking correction may materially improve containment; alternatively, product/feed controls may still be required even with correct tracking.

## Step 9 — Establish residual containment duty and install/adapt side skirting / spill sealing where required

Where representative operation still demonstrates a residual lateral-containment requirement, apply the W10 functional principle in a Sample-Conveyor-specific arrangement:

> **Keep the product on the belt using a belt-compatible flexible side-skirt / spill-seal element acting at the carrying edge rather than attempting to catch product after it has escaped.**

The Sample Conveyor arrangement is not assumed to be identical to W10. Final design must account for:

- the narrow Sample Conveyor width and trough geometry;
- corrected belt tracking range;
- actual product-size range;
- loading distribution;
- local covers/guides and installation space;
- risk of trapping large product between the skirt and belt;
- avoidance of harmful belt rubbing/gouging or skirt-induced steering;
- inspection, adjustment, replacement and clean-out access; and
- the selected OEM skirt/seal system.

Exact skirt height, length, material, support arrangement, clearance/contact condition, contact pressure and wear-adjustment detail remain OEM/site detailed-design matters.

## Step 10 — Final representative operating validation

After the required primary corrections and any secondary containment modifications, verify the complete operating relationship:

> **feed → product size/distribution → loading position → belt tracking → containment performance**

The final success question is:

> **Can the Sample Conveyor transport the required product quantity and product-size distribution while maintaining acceptable belt tracking and retaining the material within the intended carrying path under representative operation?**

Any remaining material-loss condition should then be investigated against the verified variables rather than assumed to have the same cause as the original condition.

# 3. Functional solution conclusion

The Sample Conveyor response is therefore not one isolated modification. It is a controlled hierarchy:

> **Establish design basis → validate feed-control capability → verify product-size suitability → verify loading centring/distribution → quantify and correct tracking/interference → reassess corrected carrying geometry → determine any remaining feed/product controls → define residual containment duty → install/adapt belt-compatible side skirting where required → verify final performance.**

This hierarchy applies to both Sample Conveyor 2 and Sample Conveyor 3, but the detailed corrective actions may differ because their observed evidence and local geometry are not identical.

The functional concept is now sufficiently mature for later report integration. Detailed fabrication/OEM design and quantitative site verification remain open by design and do not prevent closure of this re-evaluation stage.

# 5. Report-language / section-style control — binding reminder

When this context is later converted into the V0.6 reader-facing report, **do not copy the internal diagnostic wording mechanically**. The report text must follow the already accepted language/style controls for the relevant section.

Binding reader-facing voice:

> **Formal engineering narrative — technically rigorous, measured and explanatory, without sounding legalistic, forensic, bureaucratic or unnecessarily categorical.**

The established **softer V0.2-derived style** remains mandatory for the main report narrative. “Softer” applies to delivery, not to evidence strength, technical meaning or required action.

Preferred forms include:

- **“The evidence indicates…”**
- **“The inspection identified…”**
- **“The observed condition suggests…”**
- **“Taken together, these findings indicate…”**
- **“This is important because…”**
- **“Further measurement is required to establish…”**
- **“The available evidence does not establish…”**
- **“The recommended response is therefore…”**

For the Sample Conveyor sections specifically, prefer wording such as:

> **The photographs indicate that the observed quantity, distribution and size of the conveyed product occupy a substantial proportion of the available narrow-belt carrying geometry. The evidence also identifies lateral belt-offset conditions that can reduce the usable carrying margin. Further verification is therefore required to establish the intended feed/product-size duty, the adequacy of the existing feed-control arrangement, the causes of the belt offset and the residual containment requirement before the final side-skirt arrangement is determined.**

Avoid categorical reader-facing statements such as:

- “the conveyor is overloaded” unless quantitatively verified;
- “mistracking caused the spillage” where chronology/root cause remains unproven;
- “the product is too large” until the intended allowable size/duty is established; or
- “install skirting to fix the problem” without retaining the required primary-verification sequence.

More formal verification/close-out language may be used where appropriate in Part XI, success criteria, implementation controls and technical appendices, while still preserving evidence qualification.

Terminology control also remains binding:

- describe material/process paths directly;
- use **envelope** only where it describes a genuine measurable movement/clearance/operating boundary.
