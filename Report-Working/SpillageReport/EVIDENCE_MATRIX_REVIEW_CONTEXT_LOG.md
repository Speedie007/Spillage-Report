## CV09 — Cleaner Location, Chute Containment and Downstream Fines Handling

### Inspector clarification
`CV09-REF-008` is important because it shows the scraper/cleaner positioned outside the effective discharge-chute containment boundary. The removed fines therefore do not remain inherently captured within the chute and intended downstream fines-handling route. Material escaping outside that containment boundary contributes to spillage into the open plant area below, with active fallout supported by `CV09-REF-006` and wider consequence evidence including `CV09-REF-005`.

### Engineering significance
The functional requirement is not merely to remove fines from the belt. The cleaner and associated chute arrangement must:
1. maintain effective blade-to-belt contact;
2. remove residual material from the return belt; and
3. keep the removed material inside the intended chute/process envelope so it continues to the downstream fines-handling section.

`CV09-REF-008` therefore has a distinct essential narrative role beyond being another cleaner view: it explains the containment-boundary problem and supports the CV09 pilot concept.

### Qualification
The media does not quantify scraper contact pressure. Statements about inadequate positive contact pressure remain engineering/design considerations unless separately measured or directly verified. The cleaner location relative to the chute boundary and the observed fallout/consequence are the stronger visually supported points.

### Matrix-review decision
- `CV09-REF-002` — E accepted
- `CV09-REF-003` — E accepted
- `CV09-REF-006` — E accepted
- `CV09-REF-008` — E accepted
- remaining CV09 media — S / pending later review

## CV15 — Static Scraper Wear, Loss of Contact and Fines Bypass

### Inspector clarification
`CV15-REF-002` shows the fixed/static scraper mounted at the discharge-chute exit where the return side of the belt leaves the chute. The scraper is intended to retain the remaining fines within the discharge chute so that the fines stay inside the intended process stream.

During the site inspection, the inspector directly observed that the rubber scraper blades had worn down and that a clearance/gap had developed between the scraper and the belt. Because the scraper was positioned too high above the inspector to photograph the contact line clearly, the gap itself is not independently visible in `CV15-REF-002`.

The observed loss of blade-to-belt contact allowed fines to bypass the scraper and escape outside the intended chute containment. The resulting fines accumulation is represented by the subsequent CV15 evidence, particularly `CV15-REF-004`, `CV15-REF-005`, `CV15-REF-006` and `CV15-REF-008`. `CV15-REF-008` is significant because it helps show that the escaped fines are not confined to the immediate scraper area but migrate downward into the wider plant environment.

### Engineering significance
CV15 demonstrates a different physical scraper implementation from CV09 but a similar higher-order functional failure: fines are not being retained within the intended process/material-handling envelope.

The two case studies should therefore be linked carefully:
- **CV09:** cleaner/scraper positioned outside the effective chute-containment boundary, allowing removed wet fines to escape into the plant area.
- **CV15:** fixed/static scraper intended to retain fines in the chute, but blade wear and loss of contact allow fines to bypass the scraper and escape downstream/outside the intended containment.

Together they support the wider recommendation to standardise the **functional cleaner/containment requirement**, not one identical scraper design. A successful arrangement must maintain suitable blade-to-belt contact as wear develops and must direct removed/bypassing fines into the intended downstream fines-handling path rather than the surrounding plant environment.

### Evidentiary qualification
- `CV15-REF-002` visually shows the scraper arrangement/location and surrounding contamination.
- The worn-blade clearance/gap and loss of effective contact are **inspector-recorded direct observations**; the gap itself is not clearly captured photographically.
- The subsequent stills directly show the spillage consequence and distribution, but the photographs alone do not independently prove that every deposited particle originated from the scraper bypass.
- The causal chain is therefore supported by the combination of direct field observation plus the physical scraper arrangement and consequence evidence, rather than by `REF-002` alone.

### Matrix implication for current E-pass
The inspector clarification gives `CV15-REF-002` a materially stronger narrative role than the preliminary visual-only review recognised. It should be considered for promotion to **E — Essential body evidence** as the principal mechanism/configuration image for the CV15 cleaner failure.

The likely essential story sequence is:
1. `CV15-REF-002` — scraper configuration + inspector-recorded loss of blade contact/bypass mechanism;
2. `CV15-REF-004` — strong local fines/carryback consequence;
3. `CV15-REF-006` — distributed extent through the surrounding structure;
4. `CV15-REF-008` — downstream/wider plant-environment consequence, subject to final inspector review against duplication.

`CV15-REF-003` remains principally location/traceability evidence and does not by itself carry the engineering mechanism.

## Sample Conveyor 1 — Tail-End Fines, Cleaner-Function Inference and Multi-Level Consequence

### Inspector clarification
`LoadoutStationSample1-REF-002` shows a substantial dry-fines accumulation around the tail-end pulley / tail-end access area. The inspector interprets this as consistent with fines having remained on the return belt after the head-discharge region rather than being effectively removed and retained there. The actual head-end scraper/cleaner condition was not captured in the supplied Sample Conveyor 1 media, so `REF-002` does not visually prove a specific scraper defect or a specific loss of scraper contact.

The importance of `REF-002` is therefore twofold: it directly establishes a significant fines consequence at the tail-end/access area, and it supports a wider engineering inference—when considered together with the recurring cleaner/containment findings elsewhere in the inspected conveyor system—that Sample Conveyor 1 should be assessed for application of the same functional cleaner/containment philosophy.

This does **not** mean that the exact CV09 or CV15 hardware should be copied onto Sample Conveyor 1 without verification. The appropriate recommendation is to assess the local head/discharge geometry, existing cleaner condition, belt contact requirements, chute containment and downstream fines route, then apply the proven functional principle where suitable.

### Engineering significance
Sample Conveyor 1 can contribute to the broader system argument without overstating the evidence:
- **Directly proven:** substantial dry fines are present around the tail-end/access area; widespread material is retained at multiple structural elevations; coarse product reaches access grating; and major coarse/lump-product accumulations are retained within the surrounding structure.
- **Visually supported history:** adhered/dried deposits with run/drip morphology support a history of sticky/moist deposition, but do not prove active wetness at the instant photographed or identify the moisture source.
- **Engineering inference / recommendation candidate:** tail-end fines are consistent with return-side carryback and insufficient fines removal/containment at the head-discharge end. Because the head-end cleaner condition is not directly shown, the report should present this as a reasonable system-level interpretation requiring local verification, not as a visually proven scraper failure.

This reinforces the governing principle already established by CV09 and CV15: **do not merely remove fines from the belt; keep the fines inside the intended process/material-handling envelope.** Sample Conveyor 1 is a candidate for the wider functional cleaner/containment upgrade programme after local geometry and equipment-condition verification.

### Matrix implication for current E-pass
- `LoadoutStationSample1-REF-002` — keep E. It has a distinct role as substantial tail-end/access fines evidence and as a qualified bridge into the wider cleaner-function assessment.
- `LoadoutStationSample1-REF-004` — demote E → S because `REF-007` provides the same multi-level accumulation story with stronger global context.
- `LoadoutStationSample1-REF-005` — keep E for adhered/dried run/drip morphology and sticky/moist deposition history.
- `LoadoutStationSample1-REF-007` — keep E as the stronger global multi-level distribution image.
- `LoadoutStationSample1-REF-008` — keep E because coarse/lump-product spillage on access grating is a separate operational/access consequence from the tail-end fines shown in `REF-002`.
- `LoadoutStationSample1-REF-010` — keep E for major lower/adjacent structural accumulation.
- `LoadoutStationSample1-REF-011` — keep E as the principal/hero evidence of very large coarse/lump-product accumulation retained within the surrounding structure.

## CV06 — Cleaner Support Present, Scraper Blades Absent

### Inspector confirmation
The accepted CV06 E pair is `CV06-REF-004` and `CV06-REF-005`.

- `CV06-REF-004` directly shows the cleaner/scraper support bush or insert displaced from its housing.
- `CV06-REF-005` shows that the cleaner support beam/cross-shaft remains installed beneath the belt while the scraper blade elements are absent across the working belt width.

### Wording control
The final report should therefore avoid saying that the entire scraper assembly was completely missing. The stronger and more precise statement is that **the cleaner support structure remained installed, but the scraper blades were absent, leaving the cleaner non-functional as intended**.

The reason for the displaced bush and the reason/history for the missing blades remain unresolved.
