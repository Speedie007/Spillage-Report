## Purpose
This draft develops **PART IV — FINES, CARRYBACK AND CLEANER CONTAINMENT** within the inspector-approved V0.2 report architecture.

The purpose of Part IV is to establish one connected engineering story:

> recurring fines accumulation is a cross-system material-loss mechanism; cleaner condition is one control point; and effective cleaning is only complete when the removed material remains within the intended process stream.

The section deliberately separates:
- what the photographs/videos directly show;
- what the inspection record identifies as carryback or cleaner-related mechanism;
- what remains engineering interpretation;
- what requires measurement/design development before implementation.

It does **not** claim that every fines deposit visible in the evidence originated solely from carryback.

# PART IV — FINES, CARRYBACK AND CLEANER CONTAINMENT

## 8. Fines Are Being Lost Progressively Through the Conveyor Ecosystem

Fines form part of the combined product conveyed through the inspected material-handling system. Their presence on the belt is therefore not, by itself, a defect. The loss occurs when a portion of that material remains on or around the belt after the intended discharge and is subsequently deposited outside the designed process/material-handling path.

The inspection record identifies recurring **belt carryback** as a major cross-system mechanism. This is supported by the repeated distribution of fines beneath and alongside return-belt/support areas across both underground and surface conveyor sections.

The photographic evidence directly proves the physical consequence — distributed fines accumulation — while the operating-mechanism attribution requires appropriate qualification. A still photograph can show fines immediately beneath a return belt or roller and therefore strongly support the carryback interpretation; it cannot independently prove that every surrounding deposit originated solely from carryback.

This distinction is important because the report is not attempting to assign one universal cause to every visible pile. Instead, the repeated return-side distribution pattern, combined with the inspection record, demonstrates a recurring material-control problem that extends beyond isolated housekeeping locations.

### 8.1 Cross-system evidence of recurring fines accumulation

Representative examples include:

- **UC05–UC01 continuous underground route:** distributed dry-fines accumulation is directly verified at multiple locations, including large local piles beneath/adjacent to the conveyor. Other portions are comparatively clear, confirming that the condition varies along the route rather than being uniformly severe everywhere.
- **U5A:** extensive/widespread longitudinal fines accumulation is directly verified. `UG5A-REF-004` is particularly useful because the fines are accumulated immediately beneath the return-belt/roller region, strongly supporting the recorded return-side carryback mechanism while not proving that carryback is the sole origin of every surrounding deposit.
- **CV15:** extensive distributed fines/carryback accumulation around chute E-3181 is directly verified on grating, cross-bracing and structural members. The still evidence primarily shows dry/dried fines; the wet-fines condition and cleaner-effectiveness assessment remain source-recorded observations rather than independently demonstrated operating states in the still set.
- **Sample Conveyor 1:** extensive/widespread fines and mixed-product accumulation is directly verified at multiple structural levels. Adhered/run-like deposits support a history of sticky/moist material deposition, but the stills do not independently prove that all deposits were actively wet or that every deposit originated specifically from carryback.
- **CV14 and other lower-severity locations:** localised return-side fines deposits provide useful supporting evidence that the fines/carryback issue occurs across different levels of visible severity rather than only at the most severe assets.

These examples should not be presented as a repetitive asset register within this section. Their purpose is to establish recurrence and system distribution before the report moves into the cleaner-control cases.

### 8.2 Why the deposit may appear downstream of the initiating control failure

Carryback also reinforces the central audit principle established earlier in the report: the location where material is found is not necessarily the location where the initiating control deficiency exists.

Residual material can remain on the return belt after discharge and be progressively detached by belt flexing, return rollers, vibration, gravity or interaction with surrounding components. The resulting floor or structural deposit may therefore occur some distance from the head/discharge area where the opportunity for effective material removal and containment originally existed.

The engineering implication is that repeated downstream fines accumulation should not automatically be treated as a local floor-cleaning problem. The upstream discharge, cleaner condition and removed-material containment path must be considered as part of the mechanism assessment.

### 8.3 Wet and sticky fines

Where fines become wet or sticky, the control problem can become more persistent. The reviewed evidence includes wet-fines/slurry conditions at several surface locations and adhered/dried deposits elsewhere that support a history of sticky or moisture-affected material.

The report should not infer one common water source from those observations. The relevant point for Part IV is narrower: **material condition affects how readily fines adhere to the belt, cleaner components and surrounding structure, and therefore influences the cleaning and containment duty.**

### Part IV reader takeaway

> **Recurring fines accumulation is a system-level material-control issue. The evidence supports carryback as a major recurring mechanism, but individual deposits remain qualified according to what the specific evidence can prove.**

## 9. Why Belt Cleaning Alone Is Not the Full Control Function

A belt-cleaning arrangement performs its intended material-control function only when two outcomes are achieved:

1. residual material is removed from the belt effectively; and
2. the removed material remains within the intended process/discharge path.

Failure of either function can produce continued spillage.

The inspection provides two strong but different examples: **CV06** and **CV09**.

---

### 9.1 CV06 — Cleaning Function Compromised by Physical Condition

CV06 demonstrates a direct mechanical/maintenance failure of the cleaning function.

The reviewed evidence confirms two separate physical deficiencies:

- `CV06-REF-004` directly shows the scraper-support bush/insert displaced relative to its housing; and
- `CV06-REF-005` directly shows the cleaner support beam/cross-shaft with scraper blade elements absent from the working belt width.

Taken together, these images establish that the cleaner arrangement was materially compromised and not functioning as intended in the photographed condition.

The evidence does **not** establish why the bush displaced, when or why the scraper blades were removed/lost, or whether the condition resulted from design, maintenance history, wear, an earlier failure event or a combination of factors.

Accordingly:

- the physical cleaner deficiency is **directly verified**;
- poor housing design remains a **working engineering assessment**, not a proven cause;
- maintenance/replacement history remains a **verification item**.

### Engineering significance

CV06 demonstrates the first control failure mode:

> **If the cleaner hardware is mechanically compromised or incomplete, the belt-cleaning function itself can be lost.**

The immediate requirement is therefore to restore a complete serviceable cleaner arrangement and verify the support/retention condition. CV06 does not, by itself, justify a separate bespoke cleaner redesign before the wider cleaner/containment philosophy is developed and tested.

### Evidence-use rule

The two principal images are sufficient in the report body:
- `CV06-REF-004` — displaced support bush;
- `CV06-REF-005` — scraper blades absent from the working belt width.

Additional context photographs add little to the mechanism and should remain in the evidence register unless required for orientation.

---

### 9.2 CV09 — Cleaner / Containment Interface Failure

CV09 demonstrates a different material-control problem.

The reviewed evidence directly confirms:

- wet-fines/slurry accumulation below the conveyor/transfer area (`CV09-REF-002`);
- a heavily contaminated cleaner/scraper assembly immediately below the belt in an open local arrangement (`CV09-REF-003`);
- extensive fines contamination on surrounding structure; and
- active wet-material fallout beneath the conveyor/transfer region during operation (`CV09-REF-006`).

The media does not isolate the exact origin of every droplet or establish the final boundaries of the complete chute geometry. It also does not determine the final cleaner type, required number of cleaning stages or detailed spring/travel arrangement.

What the evidence does establish is that the **cleaner/discharge region is associated with active wet-material loss into the surrounding plant area and is not fully contained by the local arrangement shown in the reviewed media**.

This is the second control failure mode:

> **Removing material from the belt is not sufficient if that material is then allowed to leave the intended process stream.**

The engineering function must therefore consider the cleaner and the local containment envelope together.

### Engineering significance

CV09 is the strongest local case for developing the report-wide cleaner/containment philosophy because it connects:

- wet/sticky fines service;
- the cleaner position and condition;
- active fallout;
- the physical consequence below the conveyor; and
- an identifiable intended process route into which removed fines should be returned.

The evidence supports the **need for an improved cleaner/containment function**. It does not convert the conceptual chute-extension or self-tensioning-cleaner proposal into a final design.

### Evidence-use rule

Use the minimum evidence set required to prove the sequence:

1. `CV09-REF-003` — principal cleaner/containment geometry and condition image;
2. `CV09-REF-002` — principal wet-fines consequence image;
3. `CV09-REF-006` — principal dynamic evidence that wet-material fallout was active;
4. `CV09-REF-008` — supplementary geometry/operating context only if it materially improves reader understanding.

This avoids repeating the same conclusion across multiple similar photographs.

---

### 9.3 What CV06 and CV09 Prove Together

CV06 and CV09 should be read as complementary cases rather than as two versions of the same defect.

| Case | What is directly established | Material-control lesson |
|---|---|---|
| **CV06** | Cleaner support bush displaced; scraper blades absent from working belt width | The cleaning function can be lost when the cleaner is mechanically compromised or incomplete |
| **CV09** | Wet-fines accumulation, contaminated/open cleaner region and active wet-material fallout | Cleaning and containment must operate as one function so removed material remains inside the process |

Taken together, the cases support a broader engineering conclusion:

> **A cleaner is not successful merely because hardware is installed at the belt. The system must maintain the cleaning function and must control where the removed material goes.**

This conclusion is strong enough to justify a controlled pilot and an applicability review. It is **not** sufficient to state that every inaccessible conveyor has the same defect or requires identical hardware.

## 10. Cleaner / Fines-Control Philosophy and CV09 Pilot

The proposed control philosophy should standardise the **required function**, not a universal equipment layout.

The required function is:

> **Remove residual fines from the belt effectively, maintain that cleaning function through normal wear/adjustment, and keep the removed material within the intended process stream.**

CV09 is the preferred pilot location because the local evidence demonstrates the cleaner/containment problem directly enough to support controlled engineering development and because the intended destination of the removed fines can be identified within the process arrangement.

### 10.1 Pilot design-development requirements

Before fabrication or installation, verify:

- existing cleaner type, position and blade/contact condition;
- cleaner-to-belt geometry and available adjustment/travel;
- local chute/enclosure geometry and available physical envelope;
- intended route for removed fines back into the process stream;
- material condition under representative service;
- maintenance, inspection, replacement and adjustment access;
- guarding/trapping requirements;
- practical as-found baseline for downstream carryback and local fallout.

The design-development phase may evaluate:

- a spring-loaded/self-tensioning cleaner principle;
- positive wear/travel limitation;
- primary/secondary cleaning stages where justified;
- revised/extended chute containment around the cleaner;
- controlled routing of removed fines into the intended CV09 discharge stream.

These remain **engineering concepts until the geometry, cleaner selection and site constraints are verified**.

### 10.2 Pilot success should be functional, not cosmetic

The pilot should not be judged only by whether the belt or floor looks cleaner immediately after installation.

The success criteria should demonstrate that:

- downstream carryback is materially reduced relative to the pre-trial condition;
- wet fines removed at the cleaner remain within the intended process stream;
- cleaner-to-belt contact remains stable through the agreed wear range;
- no unacceptable belt damage or abnormal cleaner wear is introduced;
- inspection, adjustment and replacement access remain practical;
- no unacceptable trapping/guarding conflict is introduced;
- the monitoring period and records are sufficient to support a defensible wider-applicability decision.

The detailed staged action and authority boundaries are carried forward into **Part XI — Recommended Action Programme**.

### 10.3 Wider applicability after the pilot

If the CV09 pilot proves the required function, other relevant discharge points should be surveyed to determine whether the same **functional control philosophy** is applicable.

That review should consider:

- actual local spillage/carryback mechanism;
- cleaner presence and maintenance condition;
- belt/material condition;
- chute/discharge geometry;
- available installation envelope;
- route for removed material to return to the process;
- maintenance and guarding requirements.

The report must remain explicit:

> **This is not a recommendation to install one identical scraper at every conveyor. It is a recommendation to standardise the required material-control function and adapt the physical solution to each applicable location.**

U4 remains a clear example of why this qualification is necessary: its cleaner arrangement is materially different and requires separate local assessment.

## 10.4 Transition to Coarse / Combined-Product Loss

Part IV establishes the fines/carryback control problem, but not all observed spillage can be explained by return-side fines redistribution.

At W10 and the Sample Conveyors, significant coarse/lump or mixed product is also visible outside the intended carrying path. Those cases require a different control hierarchy centred on the **carrying envelope** — feed, loading, belt position and location-specific secondary containment.

The report therefore transitions from the return-side fines-control problem to the next question:

> **What happens when the product leaves the carrying belt during transit rather than remaining on the return belt after discharge?**

That question is addressed in **Part V — Coarse / Combined-Product Loss During Transit**.

This draft deliberately reduces duplication by assigning one reader function to each section:

- **Section 8 — Explain:** establish recurring fines/carryback as the system-level mechanism with evidence qualification.

The full asset evidence remains controlled in the evidence register and appendices rather than being repeated as equal-weight mini findings inside Part IV.
