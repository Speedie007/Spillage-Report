## Purpose
This draft develops **PART V — COARSE / COMBINED-PRODUCT LOSS DURING TRANSIT** within the inspector-approved V0.2 report architecture.

Part IV established the recurring fines/carryback story. Part V deliberately changes the mechanism:

> **the material is no longer being considered primarily as residual carryback on the return belt; the concern is coarse or mixed product leaving the carrying envelope during transit.**

The section is built around two different containment cases:

1. **W10** — significant coarse/mixed-product accumulation in a confined tunnel, with the loss consequence proven but no single initiating cause established for every deposit;
2. **Sample Conveyors 2 and 3** — narrow-belt loading/containment cases where product size and available carrying width are visibly important, while the tracking evidence differs materially between the two conveyors.

The control sequence remains:

> **understand the initiating condition → correct feed/tracking contributors → use secondary containment only where still justified.**

No design-capacity overload, tracking percentage, belt offset dimension or final side-containment geometry is introduced without measurement.

# PART V — COARSE / COMBINED-PRODUCT LOSS DURING TRANSIT

## 11. The Containment Problem Is Not Limited to Fines

Part IV showed how residual fines can remain on the return belt after discharge and be progressively redistributed downstream. The inspection also identified a separate class of material loss: **coarse/lump and mixed combined product leaving the carrying belt before the intended transfer or discharge point.**

This is a different containment failure. It cannot be explained by return-side carryback alone and therefore requires a different control hierarchy.

The carrying side of a conveyor has a finite usable material envelope created by the relationship between:
- belt width and trough geometry;
- the position of the belt relative to its support structure;
- product size and loading profile;
- feed trajectory and quantity;
- transfer/chute geometry;
- the available lateral containment margin;
- and the operating condition of the conveyor at the time.

When product leaves that envelope, the correct response depends on **why the margin was lost**. A side barrier or skirt may reduce the consequence at a suitable location, but it should not automatically become the first or only corrective action where feed, alignment, loading or geometry remain unresolved.

The two principal Part V case groups — W10 and the Sample Conveyors — demonstrate why this distinction matters.

## 12. W10 — Coarse / Mixed-Product Loss in a Restricted Tunnel

### 12.1 What the evidence establishes

The W10 photographic record directly confirms substantial dry lump/mixed-product and fines accumulation within the enclosed conveyor tunnel. Material is present beneath and adjacent to the conveyor and along the narrow side-access route.

The strongest selected views show locally severe accumulations, but the controlled asset-level classification remains **localised to moderate** across the inspected W10 evidence set. The report should therefore preserve both statements:

- individual locations can have a significant local consequence; and
- the available evidence does not justify describing the full W10 route as uniformly severe or widespread.

`W10-REF-002` provides the strongest direct evidence of coarse/mixed-product accumulation occupying the access route, while `W10-REF-010` or `W10-REF-011` provides the clearest context for the narrow enclosed tunnel and restricted maintenance/housekeeping environment.

### 12.2 What the evidence does not establish

The visible deposits do **not** isolate one definitive initiating cause for every item of W10 spillage.

Fines coating and return-side deposits are consistent with carryback, but the coarse/mixed-product accumulation cannot simply be assigned to the same mechanism without further investigation. The current evidence also does not establish:
- a measured belt mistracking condition as the sole W10 cause;
- a measured loading/trajectory defect;
- a specific chute defect responsible for all deposits;
- or a final side-containment geometry.

The correct engineering position is therefore:

> **W10 proves loss of product from the intended transport envelope and a significant restricted-access consequence; the initiating contributors still require verification.**

### 12.3 Why the tunnel context changes the consequence

W10 is important not only because product has left the carrying path, but because the conveyor runs through a confined access route.

In an open area, a local pile may primarily create a housekeeping burden. In the W10 tunnel, the same type of loss also reduces already limited access space, complicates inspection and manual recovery, and can create repeated interference with maintenance access.

This means the corrective philosophy should consider both:

1. **source reduction** — prevent or reduce the initiating loss where practicable; and
2. **secondary retention** — where justified, prevent displaced product from migrating into the access route.

The second function does not replace the first.

### 12.4 W10 source-control investigation

Before a local barrier/rail is developed as a final solution, the following should be verified:
- normal belt tracking envelope through the relevant section;
- belt/trough/support geometry;
- available belt-edge and structural clearances;
- likely source locations for coarse/mixed-product loss;
- contribution of return-side/carryback fines versus carrying-side product loss;
- loading/transfer conditions relevant to the affected zone;
- housekeeping and clean-out access requirements.

Where a correctable initiating contributor is identified, that condition should be addressed before or together with any secondary containment.

### 12.5 W10 secondary side-containment concept

A local side barrier/rail remains a reasonable **feasibility concept** because of the confined access consequence, but its purpose must be correctly defined.

The barrier should not act as a normal belt-contact or belt-training device. Its intended function would be to provide secondary retention for product that moves laterally beyond the preferred carrying zone, helping prevent that product from entering the narrow access route.

Any final concept must therefore demonstrate that it:
- remains outside the verified normal belt tracking envelope;
- does not become a routine rubbing/contact surface;
- does not trap product in a manner that creates belt, fire, wear or maintenance risk;
- can be safely cleaned and inspected;
- preserves practical conveyor and access-route maintenance;
- has structurally suitable attachment/support where required.

### 12.6 W10 action link

Part XI therefore treats W10 as:

> **source-control review + geometry verification + secondary side-containment feasibility**, not as a predetermined barrier-installation project.

The action is only closed when the initiating contributors have been investigated and the selected control demonstrates a material reduction in access-route accumulation without introducing a new belt-interaction hazard.

## 13. Sample Conveyors — Product Size, Feed and Limited Carrying Width

The Sample Conveyors present a different coarse-product containment problem from W10.

At Sample Conveyors 2 and 3, the evidence repeatedly shows large/coarse product carried on relatively narrow belts. Individual lumps occupy a substantial proportion of the available carrying width, and material is visible outside the intended belt path.

This establishes a practical containment concern, but it does **not** by itself establish that either conveyor is operating above a quantified design-capacity limit. The report therefore uses the observed relationship between product size/loading and usable carrying width as the starting engineering question rather than making an unsupported overload claim.

The common control hierarchy is:

1. **understand and control the feed/load;**
2. **verify and correct belt tracking/alignment where required;**
3. **only then finalise secondary side containment if a residual risk remains.**

This order matters because a fixed containment system should not be designed around an unresolved primary feed or tracking problem.

---

### 13.1 Sample Conveyor 2 — Feed / Loading Concern Without Proven Significant Mistracking

#### What the evidence establishes

The Sample Conveyor 2 photographs strongly confirm:
- extensive coarse/lump-product spillage around and beneath the conveyor;
- large product occupying a substantial proportion of the narrow belt carrying width;
- local containment loss through/around the enclosure and outside the intended carrying path.

`LoadoutStationSample2-REF-004` provides the strongest direct product-size-versus-belt-width evidence. `REF-006` or `REF-007` provides strong containment/loading context, and `REF-001` or `REF-003` demonstrates the wider spillage consequence.

#### Critical evidence boundary

The evidence does **not** provide clear independent proof of significant Sample Conveyor 2 mistracking.

Accordingly, Sample Conveyor 2 must not be described as though tracking has already been established as the primary cause. The correct sequence is to verify live tracking as one investigation item while keeping the directly supported feed/loading/containment concern at the centre of the case.

The evidence also does not establish:
- actual live feed rate;
- design-capacity overload;
- the final upstream metering/throttling method;
- a final side-skirt height, length or clearance.

#### Engineering response

The primary next step is to quantify the actual operating relationship between incoming product and available carrying width:
- measure usable belt width;
- document representative product size and feed condition;
- identify the existing upstream feed-control/metering arrangement;
- observe representative feed quantity and trajectory;
- verify live tracking/alignment without presuming a defect;
- establish the belt-edge operating clearance required for any later containment concept.

If feed control materially reduces the loss, the need and duty of the secondary side containment may reduce accordingly.

#### Secondary containment

A fixed side support with a flexible skirt/retention element remains a working concept only. Any previously discussed approximate dimensions remain conceptual and must not be carried into the report as verified design values.

If the control remains justified after feed/tracking verification, the final arrangement should:
- maintain suitable non-contact belt clearance through the verified operating envelope;
- retain coarse product without becoming a routine rubbing surface;
- allow cleaning and inspection;
- preserve access to belt, rollers and relevant components;
- avoid trapping product in a manner that creates a new operating or maintenance problem.

#### Sample Conveyor 2 action link

The case therefore remains:

> **verify and control feed/loading first; verify tracking as an open item; then determine the residual need for secondary containment.**

### 13.2 Sample Conveyor 3 — Feed / Loading Concern Plus Visually Supported Mistracking

Sample Conveyor 3 shares the same narrow-belt/product-size concern as Sample Conveyor 2, but the evidence adds an important second mechanism.

#### What the evidence establishes

The Sample Conveyor 3 photographs directly support:
- substantial coarse/lump-product spillage;
- large product occupying much of the narrow carrying width;
- product accumulated outside the intended belt path;
- substantial surrounding accumulation on grating/structure;
- a visually supported lateral belt-offset/mistracking condition.

`LoadoutStationSample3-REF-005` or `-006` provides strong product-size/loading evidence. `REF-002` demonstrates direct containment loss, `REF-012` demonstrates the wider consequence, and `REF-013` provides the strongest still-image support for the lateral tracking concern.

#### Tracking qualification

The lateral offset is visually supported, but its magnitude remains **unmeasured**.

The earlier approximate description that roughly half of the usable width may have been lost should not be used as a measured percentage unless repeatable dimensional verification is completed. The photographs also do not establish whether the offset is constant, intermittent or load-dependent.

The root mechanical contributor is likewise not established by the still evidence. Potential contributors such as:
- head/tail alignment;
- belt tensioning;
- idler/support condition;
- trapped accumulated product;
- or other local mechanical influences

remain inspection hypotheses until verified.

#### Engineering response

The required sequence is therefore stronger than at Sample Conveyor 2:

1. measure usable carrying width and representative product condition;
2. identify and assess the upstream feed-control mechanism;
3. observe representative feed quantity/trajectory;
4. quantify the lateral belt offset;
5. inspect head/tail alignment, belt tension and relevant support/idler condition;
6. remove trapped accumulated product before final tracking assessment;
7. correct or control the verified feed/tracking contributors;
8. re-evaluate the residual containment requirement;
9. only then freeze any secondary side-containment geometry.

#### Secondary containment

As with Sample Conveyor 2, side containment is a **secondary control** rather than a substitute for correcting feed or tracking.

The final system, if still required after correction, should be based on the verified belt operating envelope and should remain clear of the belt during normal operation while providing practical product retention, access and clean-out capability.

#### Sample Conveyor 3 action link

The case therefore becomes:

> **feed/loading control + quantitative tracking investigation/correction + residual secondary containment**, in that order.

### 13.3 Why Sample Conveyor 2 and Sample Conveyor 3 must not be merged into one finding

The two conveyors should be discussed together because they share the narrow-belt/product-size problem, but they should not be given identical causal wording.

| Engineering question | Sample Conveyor 2 | Sample Conveyor 3 |
|---|---|---|
| Coarse product occupies substantial belt width | Directly supported | Directly supported |
| Containment loss / surrounding spillage | Directly supported | Directly supported |
| Quantified design overload | Not established | Not established |
| Significant mistracking | **Not visually established / remains unconfirmed** | **Visually supported; magnitude unmeasured** |
| Root tracking cause | Not established / only verify if present | Not established; requires mechanical investigation |
| First control priority | Feed/loading verification | Feed/loading + tracking verification/correction |
| Secondary containment | Consider after verification | Consider after primary corrections |

This distinction is a useful example of the report's evidence discipline: **similar consequences do not justify identical causal conclusions.**

## 13.4 Part V Control Philosophy

Taken together, W10 and the Sample Conveyor cases reinforce a common engineering rule:

> **Secondary containment should retain material that would otherwise escape, but it should not be used to hide an unresolved primary loading, tracking or geometry problem.**

The correct sequence is:

> **identify the loss mechanism → verify the operating geometry/feed condition → correct the primary contributor → determine the residual containment duty → design the secondary control from measured conditions → prove performance in operation.**

This also explains why one universal side-barrier or skirt design is inappropriate across the audit scope. W10 operates in a restricted tunnel and may require a local non-contact retention barrier. The Sample Conveyors have narrow carrying widths and a feed/loading problem; Sample Conveyor 3 additionally has a tracking concern. The required material-control function is common, but the hardware and geometry must remain location-specific.

## 13.5 Transition to Part VI — Tracking as a Material-Containment Control

Part V demonstrates that a conveyor can lose coarse product when the available carrying envelope becomes inadequate for the material being transported. One way that usable carrying margin can be reduced is **lateral belt displacement**.

Sample Conveyor 3 provides the first Part V example: the belt is visibly offset relative to the local guide/trainer arrangement, but the magnitude and root cause remain unresolved.

The next part therefore isolates tracking/alignment as its own material-control mechanism and examines two deliberately different cases:

- **U4 Tripper Car**, where mistracking and one-sided product loss are strongly supported and the alignment condition requires quantitative verification; and
- **L3**, where a tail-end offset is supported but the current evidence does not establish the same direct causal chain to significant solid-product loss.

This contrast allows the report to move from:

> **coarse-product containment consequence**

into:

> **how belt position itself can reduce the usable carrying envelope — and why the evidence strength must determine the causal conclusion.**
