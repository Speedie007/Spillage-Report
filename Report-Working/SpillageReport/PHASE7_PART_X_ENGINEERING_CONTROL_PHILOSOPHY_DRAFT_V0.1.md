This draft develops **PART X — ENGINEERING CONTROL PHILOSOPHY** within the inspector-approved V0.2 report architecture.

Part IX established the consolidated engineering interpretation:

> the inspected system has a connected material- and process-containment problem expressed through several different mechanisms.

Part X converts that interpretation into one governing engineering philosophy before the report moves into the detailed action programme.

It is intentionally **not** a second recommendations chapter. It defines:
- the common control objective;
- the order in which controls should be considered;

- what should and should not be standardised;
- and what constitutes functional proof that a control has worked.

The detailed asset actions, dependencies and acceptance criteria remain in Part XI.

# PART X — THE ENGINEERING CONTROL PHILOSOPHY

## 23. Keep the Combined Product Inside the Process

The audit findings can be reduced to one higher-order engineering objective:

> **Keep the combined product — fines and coarse/lump ore — within the intended process/material-handling envelope throughout transit, and keep process water within its intended operating envelope, using controls appropriate to the specific failure mechanism and local geometry.**

This objective is deliberately broader than “improve housekeeping”, “replace scrapers” or “install more skirting”.

Those actions may form part of the solution at individual locations, but none of them is universally applicable because the audit has demonstrated several different loss mechanisms.

The governing question for each condition is therefore:

> **Where should the product or process medium be at this stage, how is it leaving that intended envelope, and what control best prevents or contains that specific loss?**

---

## 23.1 Control the Source Before Managing the Consequence

The preferred control sequence is:

1. **identify the actual loss mechanism;**
2. **verify the relevant operating condition and geometry;**
3. **correct the primary source or contributor where practicable;**
4. **provide primary containment appropriate to the process duty;**
5. **add secondary containment where a residual loss risk remains and the geometry justifies it;**
6. **retain housekeeping/recovery as consequence control;**
7. **prove performance under representative operation before close-out.**

This sequence does not mean every case requires all seven steps. It provides the decision hierarchy.

For example:
- a missing cleaner blade should first be restored as a maintenance condition;
- a mistracked belt should not be “contained” by allowing a side barrier to become a normal rubbing surface;
- a screen-water leak should not be enclosed around an assumed source before the escape mechanism is identified;
- a trip/restart deposit may require procedural containment rather than mechanical redesign.

The central principle remains:

> **Housekeeping controls the consequence. Source control should reduce the recurrence.**

---

## 23.2 Fines / Carryback — Clean the Belt and Keep the Fines in the Process

Fines are part of the conveyed combined product. The control objective is not to eliminate fines from the material stream prematurely; it is to stop them escaping the intended handling process.

For carryback, an effective control therefore requires two linked functions:

1. **remove residual material from the belt effectively;** and
2. **retain the removed material within the intended process/discharge path.**

CV06 and CV09 demonstrate why both functions matter.

CV06 shows that the cleaning function can be lost through equipment condition. CV09 shows that even where material is being removed, the overall material-control function remains incomplete if the removed wet fines can fall outside the effective discharge containment.

The report-wide fines-control philosophy is therefore:

> **Do not merely clean the belt. Keep the fines inside the process.**

### Functional requirements
At applicable discharge locations, the engineered solution should consider:
- cleaner effectiveness under the actual belt/material condition;
- maintenance of suitable cleaner-to-belt contact through the intended wear range;
- controlled blade travel/adjustment as required by the selected design;
- containment of removed material within the intended process envelope;
- a defined route for removed fines to report back into the process;
- safe inspection, adjustment and replacement access;
- compatibility with guarding and surrounding equipment;
- verification under representative operating conditions.

### Standardise the function, not automatically the hardware
The CV09 pilot is intended to prove the **functional cleaner-and-containment philosophy**.

A successful pilot would justify structured assessment of other relevant discharge points. It would not establish that one cleaner model, blade arrangement or chute geometry should be copied unchanged throughout the mine.

U4 is an explicit example where the cleaner configuration and local geometry differ materially and require a separate assessment.

---

## 23.3 Coarse / Lump Product — Preserve the Carrying Envelope

Coarse/lump product presents a different containment duty.

The control objective is to keep the product within the usable carrying envelope from loading until the intended transfer/discharge point.

That envelope depends on the combined relationship between:
- usable belt width and trough profile;
- product size and distribution;
- incoming feed quantity and trajectory;
- belt tracking/centring;
- local transfer geometry;
- lateral clearance;
- and any existing side-containment system.

The control hierarchy for coarse-product loss should therefore be:

1. **verify the incoming load and trajectory;**
2. **verify the usable belt carrying geometry;**
3. **verify and correct tracking/alignment where required;**
4. **determine the residual lateral-containment duty;**
5. **develop secondary skirts, rails or barriers only from the verified operating envelope.**

This sequence is particularly important at the Sample Conveyors and W10.

### Secondary containment must remain secondary
A side skirt, rail or barrier can be valuable where product can still escape after the primary operating condition is controlled.

It should not be used to mask an unresolved mechanical or loading defect, and it should not become a routine belt-contact surface unless specifically engineered for such a function.

Any secondary containment should be assessed for:
- normal belt tracking envelope;
- clearances;
- trapping of product;
- impact/wear duty;
- safe clean-out;
- maintenance access;
- structural attachment;
- guarding and operational interaction.

---

## 23.4 Belt Tracking / Alignment — Preserve Margin Before Adding Containment

Tracking is both a mechanical and a material-containment requirement.

A laterally displaced belt reduces the usable carrying margin on one side. Where the belt is heavily loaded or the local containment envelope is narrow, this can directly contribute to product loss.

The correct control is therefore not simply to add more material beside the belt.

Where a tracking concern is supported, the preferred sequence is:

1. establish fixed reference points;
2. measure the relevant pulley/support/belt-path geometry;
3. identify the actual mechanical contributor;
4. correct the confirmed contributor;
5. observe the belt under representative operation;
6. verify the required operating range, including moving equipment where applicable;
7. retain an as-left baseline for future trending.

The evidence strength still governs the causal statement.

U4 supports a strong tracking-to-one-sided-product-loss relationship. L3 supports an alignment concern without an equivalent proven solid-product loss chain. The engineering philosophy applies to both, but the report does not pretend the two evidence cases are identical.

---

## 23.5 Transfer Points — Keep Material Inside the Intended Chute / Process Envelope

At transfer and discharge locations, product changes direction and may change velocity, trajectory or condition. These points are therefore natural containment boundaries.

The control objective is to ensure that material follows the intended transfer path and that any removed carryback or displaced material reports to a controlled process destination rather than to surrounding structure or access areas.

Where transfer-point loss is confirmed or suspected, the engineering assessment should consider the relevant combination of:
- feed and discharge trajectory;
- chute geometry;
- local sealing;
- blockages or restrictions;
- material condition;
- belt position;
- cleaner location;
- and the route available for removed or redirected material.

No single transfer modification should be prescribed until the local mechanism and geometry are established.

CV09 is the principal example in this report of why the **cleaner and surrounding discharge containment envelope must be designed as one functional system**.

---

## 23.6 Process Water — Keep Water and Entrained Fines Inside the Screening / Washing Process

The same containment principle applies to process water.

Water that is intended to remain within the screen/chute process should not repeatedly escape into surrounding plant areas where it can carry fines, wet structures and expand the housekeeping burden.

The control hierarchy is:

1. confirm active loss of containment;
2. identify the actual escape path/mechanism;
3. inspect the relevant seals, interfaces, movement/clearance and enclosure geometry;
4. develop the containment modification from the verified mechanism;
5. prove the corrected condition during representative operation.

At the Screens, active water escape is already verified. The detailed physical correction remains dependent on safe shutdown investigation.

The structural support assessment remains a separate workstream. Containing water does not by itself establish the remaining capacity or repair requirement of already deteriorated/cut members.

---

## 23.7 Structural Integrity Is Part of Maintaining a Controlled Process Envelope

Material containment relies on the supporting equipment and structures remaining suitable for their intended duty.

Where severe corrosion, section loss, concrete deterioration or physical modification of support members is present, the audit cannot treat the issue only as a cleanliness or spillage consequence.

The appropriate control is competent assessment of the affected structure, including the dimensions, section loss, connections, damage geometry and load-path information required to determine remaining capacity and repair/replacement scope.

This applies particularly to:
- the cut/notched/corroded screen support region; and
- the deteriorated concrete/steel interfaces at L1.

The report does not infer remaining structural capacity from photographs and does not assign unverified causal histories to the damage.

---

## 23.8 Event-Driven Product Handling — Keep Necessary Intervention Controlled

Not all product that leaves the normal belt path is uncontrolled normal-operation spillage.

Where product must be deliberately removed during trip/restart preparation or another authorised intervention, the containment philosophy becomes operational:

- remove only under the applicable authorised procedure;
- use a controlled temporary placement or containment arrangement;
- preserve safe access and equipment clearance;
- define responsibility for recovery;
- return the material to the process through an agreed route;
- distinguish event-driven material from recurring source-generated spillage.

The objective is not to eliminate necessary intervention. It is to prevent necessary intervention from creating unmanaged secondary spillage.

---

## 23.9 Housekeeping — Support the Engineering Controls and Measure Their Effect

Housekeeping remains necessary even after engineering controls improve.

Some residual loss, planned intervention or maintenance-related material recovery may still occur. The role of housekeeping is to:
- restore safe access;
- recover escaped product;
- prevent accumulation around equipment and structures;
- maintain visibility for inspection;
- prevent deposits from obscuring or aggravating other conditions.

It should also become a feedback mechanism.

If a source-control intervention is effective, recurring accumulation under comparable conditions should reduce. Repeat housekeeping demand can therefore be used qualitatively, and where South32 chooses quantitatively, as part of post-intervention monitoring.

Housekeeping is therefore both:
- **a necessary consequence control**, and
- **a useful indicator of whether the source-control programme is succeeding.**

---

## 23.10 Primary, Secondary and Consequence Controls

The audit philosophy can be summarised through three levels of control.

### Primary / source control
Controls the mechanism at or near the point where containment is first lost.

Examples include:
- restoring effective cleaner function;
- correcting belt alignment/tracking;
- controlling feed/trajectory;
- correcting the verified screen-water escape mechanism.

### Secondary containment
Captures or restricts residual loss after the primary condition has been addressed or where some residual containment duty remains inherent to the geometry/process.

Examples may include:
- improved chute/enclosure around a cleaner;
- local skirting;
- W10 side barrier/rail where justified;
- controlled temporary laydown/containment for event-driven product removal.

### Consequence control
Manages material after it has escaped or after a necessary intervention.

Examples include:
- housekeeping;
- product recovery;
- access clearing;
- temporary operational controls pending permanent correction.

The hierarchy is not intended to imply that secondary or consequence controls are unimportant. It ensures that they do not substitute silently for correction of a practicable primary source mechanism.

---

## 23.11 Standardise Engineering Requirements, Adapt Physical Solutions

A major conclusion of the audit is that South32 can pursue consistency without forcing identical hardware into different geometries.

The items that can be standardised at system level are the **required outcomes and engineering questions**, for example:
- product remains inside the intended process envelope;
- cleaners maintain the required function through the agreed service condition;
- removed material has a controlled process destination;
- belts maintain suitable tracking/carrying margin;
- secondary containment remains compatible with the verified operating envelope;
- process water remains within the intended screening/washing system;
- structural modifications are formally assessed and approved;
- actions are verified under representative operation before close-out.

The physical solution can then be adapted to:
- local belt and material duty;
- geometry;
- available installation envelope;
- existing equipment;
- access and maintenance requirements;
- guarding/safety constraints;
- OEM and site requirements.

This is the distinction between **standardising the control philosophy** and **standardising hardware**.

---

## 23.12 Functional Close-Out — Prove the Mechanism Has Been Controlled

The audit recommendations should not be regarded as complete merely because a maintenance task has been signed off, a component has been replaced, a barrier has been installed or accumulated material has been removed.

The preferred close-out sequence is:

> **As-found evidence → verification / measurement → corrective design or control → implementation → representative operating proof → as-left record → monitoring / closure.**

The level of proof should be appropriate to the action.

Examples include:
- CV09: demonstrate reduced downstream carryback and controlled routing of removed wet fines;
- U4: demonstrate stable tracking through full tripper travel after geometry correction;
- Sample Conveyors: demonstrate controlled feed/tracking and reduced coarse-product loss before final containment close-out;
- Screens: demonstrate that the verified water-escape mechanism has been corrected during operation;
- structural items: retain competent assessment and approved repair/acceptance records;
- trip/restart controls: demonstrate that temporary placement and recovery are managed through an approved procedure.

This functional close-out principle is the bridge between the audit and the implementation programme.

---

## 23.13 Engineering Control Philosophy — Final Statement

The report-wide philosophy can therefore be stated as follows:

> **Keep the product and process media within the intended handling envelope. Correct the source mechanism where it is known, verify it where it is not, add secondary containment where justified by the residual duty and local geometry, and use housekeeping/recovery to manage consequence rather than to substitute for engineering control.**

The physical solution will differ by location.

The governing objective does not.

# Transition to Part XI — Recommended Action Programme

Part X establishes **how the problem should be controlled**.

Part XI converts that philosophy into **what should be done next, in what sequence, with what verification and what functional acceptance criteria**.

The recommended programme therefore begins with the actions that can prove or correct the highest-value system and local mechanisms:

1. prove the cleaner-and-containment source-control concept at CV09;
2. correct or verify the strongest local material-loss mechanisms at U4, the Sample Conveyors and W10;
3. identify and correct screen-water escape and complete the required structural assessments;
4. extend proven controls only where the local mechanism and geometry justify them;
5. formalise operational and housekeeping governance as supporting controls.

The result is a direct evidence-to-action chain rather than a second list of findings.
