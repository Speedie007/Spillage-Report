# PART I — WHY THE AUDIT WAS UNDERTAKEN

## 1. Executive Summary
### Reader purpose
Give management the full conclusion in two to three pages without requiring them to read every detailed evidence section.

### Proposed narrative
Spillage was observed at multiple locations across the Wessels material-handling system, from the underground crusher and conveyor route through the surface screening and load-out areas. The audit was undertaken to determine whether these deposits were isolated housekeeping problems or evidence of broader material-containment failures within the process.

The audit found that there is **no single spillage mechanism**. The observed condition is produced by several interacting mechanisms, of which recurring fines carryback is the dominant system-wide pattern. Additional local mechanisms include coarse-product loss during transit, belt mistracking/alignment, transfer and side-containment limitations, process-water escape, and event-driven material removal following conveyor trips.

A central audit finding is that **the location where spilled material accumulates is not necessarily the location where the initiating failure occurs**. Fines may remain adhered to the return belt after discharge and be removed progressively by downstream rollers; water may transport fines away from the original escape point; mistracking may develop before material is lost; and trip/restart clearing may place material away from the initiating event.

Taken together, the evidence indicates that the most effective long-term strategy is not increased housekeeping alone. The higher-order engineering objective is to **keep the combined product inside the intended process envelope while it is in transit**, and to control the specific mechanism by which that envelope is being breached.

This means:
- fines should be removed from the belt effectively **and retained within the intended process stream**;
- coarse/lump product should be kept within the usable belt carrying envelope through suitable loading, tracking and local lateral containment;
- transfer points should retain material within the chute/process envelope;
- process water should remain within the screening/washing process;
- event-driven material removal should be controlled, contained and recovered through defined operating procedures.

CV09 is recommended as the principal pilot location for development of an improved cleaner-and-containment philosophy. The objective is not to install identical hardware everywhere, but to prove a functional control philosophy and then apply it, with location-specific engineering, at other suitable conveyor discharges.

Separate targeted actions are required at W10, the Sample Conveyors, U4, the Screens and L1, where local geometry or structural conditions create different failure mechanisms.

### Executive-summary takeaway
**The mine has a material-containment problem with several mechanisms, not simply a housekeeping problem or a collection of unrelated scraper defects.**

# PART II — UNDERSTANDING THE MATERIAL-HANDLING SYSTEM

## 5. The Material Journey
### Purpose
Before discussing defects, explain what the system is trying to do.

### Proposed narrative
The material handled by the inspected system is **combined product** comprising lump/coarse ore together with fines. Fines are already present after blasting and additional fines are generated during primary crushing and subsequent handling.

From the underground crusher onward, the conveyor system is intended to transport this combined material through successive transfer and processing stages while retaining it within the designed material path. The fine fraction is therefore not inherently a defect or contaminant at every conveyor. It is part of the product stream and should remain controlled until the process reaches the stage intended to separate, handle or dispose of it in a controlled manner.

This distinction is important. The audit problem is not simply that fines exist; the problem is that portions of the material stream are **escaping the intended process envelope before reaching the stage designed to handle them**.

The same principle applies to coarse/lump ore. Where large product falls from the carrying belt during transit, the carrying envelope has been breached even if the exact initiating event is not yet fully isolated.

### Reader takeaway
The report is about **loss of process containment during transport**, not the mere presence of fines or lump ore.

---

## 6. The Central Audit Principle — Spillage Location Is Not Always Cause Location
### Proposed narrative
One of the most important conclusions from the inspection is that visible spillage must not automatically be interpreted as a local failure at the point where the material is found.

Examples include:
- fines remaining on the return belt after discharge and being detached progressively by downstream rollers;
- wet fines/slurry being transported away from the original leakage or cleaning point;
- belt mistracking developing before material is lost at a later position;
- trip/restart clearing creating a secondary deposit beside the conveyor rather than at the point of the initiating trip.

For this reason, each finding should be read through the sequence:

`Observed deposit → material type → immediate mechanism → contributing condition → initiating cause → consequence → control.`

### Recommended visual
Simple cause-and-effect diagram rather than a dense table.

# PART III — WHAT THE AUDIT FOUND

## 7. The Overall Spillage Picture
### Opening narrative
The audit identified three recurring material-loss families plus one event-driven operating mechanism:

1. fines / carryback;
2. coarse or mixed combined-product loss during transit;
3. water-related escape / wet fines / slurry;
4. event-driven trip/restart unloading.

These mechanisms overlap in some areas but require different controls. The remainder of the report explains each mechanism using selected evidence from across the system.

# PART IV — FINES, CARRYBACK AND CLEANER CONTAINMENT

## 8. Fines Are Being Lost Progressively Through the Conveyor Ecosystem
### Narrative
Recurring fines accumulation was observed across multiple surface and underground conveyors. The repeated location of deposits beneath return-belt and support-roller areas is consistent with carryback material remaining on the belt after the head discharge and being progressively detached downstream.

This means a large floor deposit beneath a return route may be the consequence of ineffective material control at the preceding discharge rather than a defect at the exact point where the deposit is found.

Wet fines are particularly persistent because moisture can increase adhesion and allow material to remain on the belt or surrounding structure for longer periods.

### Supporting system evidence
Use selected images from:
- UC05-UC01;
- U5A;
- CV15;
- Sample Conveyor 1;
- other supporting conveyors where they help show recurrence/extent.

### Reader takeaway
**Carryback is a recurring system mechanism, not an isolated conveyor event.**

---

## 9. Why Belt Cleaning Alone Is Not the Full Control Function
### Lead statement
A belt cleaner succeeds only when two functions are achieved:
1. residual material is removed from the belt;
2. the removed material remains inside the intended process/material path.

### 9.1 CV06 — Mechanical/maintenance failure example
Narrative:
CV06 demonstrates a case where the cleaner cannot perform its intended function because its physical condition is compromised. The support bush is displaced and the scraper blades are absent from the working belt width.

The visual evidence proves the defective condition. It does not establish the full failure history, so poor housing design and maintenance history remain qualified working explanations.

Use evidence:
- CV06-REF-004
- CV06-REF-005

### 9.2 CV09 — Cleaner works partly, but containment still fails
Narrative:
CV09 demonstrates a different failure. The existing cleaner is capable of removing wet carryback from the belt, but the removed material can fall outside the effective discharge containment. The belt may therefore be cleaner while the plant area becomes dirtier.

This condition is important because it shows that **belt cleaning and material containment must be treated as one engineering function**.

Use evidence:
- CV09-REF-003 — cleaner geometry
- CV09-REF-002 — wet-spillage consequence
- CV09-REF-006 — active fallout video still / reference
- additional topology/context evidence where needed

### 9.3 System implication
CV06 and CV09 should be presented together:

- CV06: cleaner function lost through condition / missing components.
- CV09: material removal occurs, but containment of removed material is inadequate.

Together they support development of a broader cleaner-and-containment control philosophy.

---

## 10. Proposed Cleaner / Fines-Control Philosophy
### Narrative
CV09 is the preferred pilot because its failure mechanism is directly demonstrated and the intended process destination for the removed fines is identifiable.

The pilot should test a functional arrangement that:
- maintains positive cleaner contact as the blade wears;
- limits cleaner travel/wear mechanically;
- directs removed wet fines into the intended CV09 fines discharge stream;
- keeps the cleaner within an effective containment envelope;
- retains safe access for inspection and maintenance;
- considers primary/secondary cleaning where justified.

If the pilot performs successfully, the **functional philosophy** should then be assessed for application at other relevant conveyor discharges.

The report must state clearly:

> This is not a recommendation to install one identical scraper at every conveyor. It is a recommendation to standardise the required material-control function and adapt the hardware to each applicable location.

U4 remains an explicit example where different geometry requires a separate assessment.

# PART V — COARSE / COMBINED-PRODUCT LOSS DURING TRANSIT

## 11. The Containment Problem Is Not Limited to Fines
### Narrative
The inspection also identified locations where significant coarse/lump or mixed product had left the carrying belt during transit. These conditions cannot be explained by return-side carryback and require a separate control philosophy.

The governing requirement is that the carrying belt should keep the product inside its usable transport envelope until the intended transfer/discharge point.

Potential contributors include:
- belt mistracking;
- loading/trajectory;
- limited usable belt width;
- transfer/chute geometry;
- insufficient lateral containment;
- event-driven operating conditions.

The correct control depends on which mechanism applies at each location.

---

## 12. W10 — Coarse Product Loss in a Confined Tunnel
### Narrative
W10 is an important case because significant coarse/mixed-product accumulation was observed outside the intended belt path in a confined tunnel environment.

The current evidence does not isolate one definitive initiating cause for every W10 deposit. The audit should therefore not claim that one specific defect is proven.

What is established is that product has left the carrying envelope and entered a restricted access zone. In a confined tunnel, even a localised loss can have a disproportionate operational consequence because the product occupies limited access space and creates repeated manual-recovery requirements.

This makes W10 a reasonable candidate for investigation of an additional **side-containment barrier/rail** concept, subject to dimensional and tracking verification.

The intent of such a barrier is not to become a normal belt-contact surface. Its purpose is to provide a secondary control that helps keep displaced coarse product within the usable carrying zone — effectively keeping the product in its intended transport lane.

### Evidence sequence
Use enough photographs to show:
1. tunnel topology / restricted access;
2. coarse product outside the belt path;
3. severity / consequence;
4. geometry relevant to a containment concept.

### Recommendation qualification
Any barrier/skirt concept requires verification of:
- normal tracking envelope;
- clearances;
- belt/trough geometry;
- structure;
- maintenance access;
- trapping/wear risks;
- loading/transfer conditions.

---

## 13. Sample Conveyors — Product Size, Feed and Limited Carrying Width
### Narrative
Sample Conveyors 2 and 3 represent another coarse-product containment problem, but the evidence suggests a different mechanism from W10.

Large/coarse product occupies a substantial proportion of the narrow belt carrying width. The first engineering question is therefore whether the incoming quantity and product-size distribution are appropriate for the usable belt width and existing feed arrangement.

The control hierarchy should be presented in order:
1. understand and control the feed/load;
2. verify/correct belt tracking and tension;
3. add secondary side-skirt/containment where appropriate.

### Sample Conveyor 2
- loading/containment concern confirmed;
- significant mistracking remains unconfirmed;
- feed control remains the primary investigation.

### Sample Conveyor 3
- loading/containment concern confirmed;
- lateral offset/mistracking visually supported;
- magnitude remains unmeasured;
- root mechanical cause remains unresolved.

### Working concept
A removable/maintainable side-support and rubber-skirt arrangement may provide additional containment after the feed/tracking condition is addressed. Preliminary dimensions remain conceptual only.

# PART VI — ALIGNMENT AND LOCAL PRODUCT-LOSS MECHANISMS

## 14. Belt Tracking as a Material-Containment Requirement
### Narrative
A belt can only use its full carrying width when it remains centred relative to the supporting structure. Lateral displacement reduces containment margin on one side and can expose product to the edge of the carrying envelope.

Tracking should therefore be treated as both a mechanical condition and a material-containment control.

---

## 15. U4 Tripper Car — Strong Local Cause-and-Effect Example
### Narrative
U4 provides one of the clearest local examples of belt mistracking associated with one-sided product loss.

The supplied evidence directly verifies:
- significant lateral belt offset/mistracking;
- coarse/lump product concentrated along one side;
- extensive local fines accumulation.

Tail-pulley misalignment was recorded during the inspection and is visually supported, but the media does not provide a fixed survey reference to quantify that misalignment.

The report should therefore distinguish:

> **mistracking and one-side product loss — visually verified**

from:

> **tail-pulley misalignment magnitude — requires quantitative verification.**

U4 also has a materially different/custom cleaner arrangement, so it should not automatically receive the general CV09 cleaner solution.

### Recommended response
- realign/verify the relevant pulley geometry;
- check tracking through representative operation and full tripper travel;
- verify tail, carrying and return paths;
- treat the U4 cleaner/containment configuration separately.

---

## 16. L3 — Alignment Concern Without a Proven Solid-Product Causal Chain
Use L3 as a deliberate contrast to U4.

Narrative:
The tail-end offset is supported by the evidence, but the current record does not establish that the offset is causing significant solid-product loss. L3 should therefore remain an alignment/tracking corrective-action item without being promoted into the same causal category as U4.

This contrast demonstrates the audit's evidence discipline: similar-looking conditions do not automatically receive the same causal conclusion.

# PART VII — WATER, WET FINES AND STRUCTURAL CONSEQUENCES

## 17. Water as a Material-Transport and Deterioration Mechanism
### Narrative
Water introduces a different form of containment problem. When process or cleaning water escapes its intended envelope, it can mobilise fines, create slurry, spread contamination away from the source, increase slip/housekeeping burden and repeatedly wet structural members.

Not every wet deposit proves the same water source. The report should distinguish directly observed process-water escape from other potential sources such as wash-down or operational hose use.

---

## 18. Screens — Confirmed Water Escape, Unresolved Equipment-Level Cause
### Narrative
Video evidence confirms active water escaping the operating screen/chute environment. The exact equipment-level mechanism could not be safely established during operation.

Therefore:
- water loss of containment is confirmed;
- the precise leak/escape mechanism remains unresolved;
- a safe shutdown inspection is required before a detailed modification is specified.

### Separate structural story
The same area contains severe corrosion/deterioration and visible cut-outs/notches in support members.

The physical condition is directly verified. The reason the members were cut is not known. Any explanation that the cuts were made for screen installation/replacement remains a working hypothesis only.

The structural condition should therefore receive its own formal assessment rather than being treated merely as a housekeeping consequence.

---

## 19. L1 — Spillage, Historical Belt Contact and Structural Deterioration
### Narrative
L1 contains several related but separate findings:

1. widespread material/fines accumulation;
2. physical evidence that the belt has previously/intermittently contacted adjacent structural supports;
3. concrete deterioration/spalling and exposed reinforcement;
4. severe steel corrosion/section loss.

The captured running belt is clear of the worn supports, so the report should not claim continuous present contact. The wear slots establish that contact has occurred.

Mistracking is the probable historical mechanism for that contact, while fines/moisture exposure is a separate contributor to the corrosive environment.

The recommendation is therefore both mechanical and structural:
- continue/verify tracking control;
- assess the affected structural components formally;
- repair/replace as defined by competent structural assessment;
- reduce recurring product/moisture accumulation around the structure.

---

# PART VIII — EVENT-DRIVEN AND OPERATIONAL SPILLAGE

## 20. Trips, Restart and Manual Product Removal
### Narrative
Not all spillage is chronic. Conveyor trips caused by blockages or mechanical/electrical events can leave a loaded belt that must be partially cleared before restart.

Where product is manually placed beside the conveyor or at the nearest safe location, the resulting deposit is an operationally generated secondary spillage event.

This mechanism should be separated from chronic carryback because the control is procedural rather than purely mechanical.

### Required clarification/action
Confirm:
- formal unloading/restart work instruction;
- authorised responsibility;
- temporary laydown/containment arrangement;
- recovery/reintroduction method and timing.

---

## 21. Housekeeping — Necessary but Not the Primary Solution
### Narrative
Manual shovelling, wheelbarrows/trolleys and water wash-down are used to manage accumulated material. These controls are necessary to restore access and operating condition, but repeated cleaning of the same locations indicates that the initiating source remains active.

The audit recommendation should therefore state clearly:

> **Housekeeping controls the consequence. Source control should reduce the recurrence.**

This distinction is central to moving from repeated cleanup toward sustainable spillage reduction.

# PART IX — WHAT THE COMBINED EVIDENCE MEANS

## 22. Consolidated Engineering Interpretation
### Proposed structure
Start with the overall conclusion, then separate systemic and local mechanisms.

### Systemic / recurring mechanisms
- fines carryback throughout the conveyor ecosystem;
- variable cleaner condition/effectiveness;
- incomplete containment of material removed by cleaners at some discharges;
- recurring housekeeping burden as a consequence of continued source loss.

### Local / geometry-specific mechanisms
- W10 coarse-product transit loss in a confined tunnel;
- Sample 2/3 loading-versus-belt-width and containment;
- Sample 3 mistracking;
- U4 alignment/mistracking;
- Screens water escape;
- L1 contact/structural deterioration;
- trip/restart unloading.

### Important conclusion
The mine does not have one spillage defect. It has a connected set of material-containment failures requiring one common **control philosophy** but several different physical solutions.

# PART X — THE ENGINEERING CONTROL PHILOSOPHY

## 23. Keep the Combined Product Inside the Process
This should be a major report section, not a short recommendation bullet.

### Core principle
> **Keep the combined product — fines and coarse/lump ore — within the intended material-handling envelope throughout transit, and manage process water within its designed envelope, using controls appropriate to the specific failure mechanism and local geometry.**

### 23.1 Fines / carryback
- clean belt effectively;
- maintain cleaner contact through wear;
- keep removed fines inside the intended process stream;
- prove the philosophy at CV09;
- survey/adapt other suitable discharge points.

### 23.2 Coarse/lump product
- verify loading/trajectory;
- maintain belt centring/tracking;
- use local side-containment/skirting/barriers where consequence and geometry justify them;
- do not use a universal hardware solution.

### 23.3 Transfer points
- keep material within chute/transfer envelope;
- address blockages, sealing or trajectory where confirmed.

### 23.4 Process water
- retain within screen/washing process;
- correct actual leak mechanism after shutdown inspection.

### 23.5 Operational events
- controlled temporary placement;
- defined recovery/reintroduction;
- avoid converting unavoidable trip clearing into uncontrolled recurring spillage.

# PART XI — RECOMMENDED ACTION PROGRAMME

## 24. Priority 1 — Prove System-Level Source-Control Concepts
### CV09 pilot
Develop, measure, design, install and monitor the cleaner/containment pilot.

Success criteria should ultimately consider:
- reduced carryback downstream;
- reduced wet fines falling outside containment;
- stable cleaner contact;
- maintainability;
- acceptable wear behaviour;

## 25. Priority 2 — Correct Confirmed Local Material-Loss Mechanisms
- U4 alignment/tracking correction and full-travel verification.
- Sample 3 tracking/feed/containment investigation.
- Sample 2 feed/loading investigation.
- W10 local side-containment feasibility investigation.

## 26. Priority 3 — Water and Structural Actions
- shutdown investigation of screen water escape;
- structural assessment of screen supports;
- L1 structural assessment/repair definition;
- manage ongoing wet fines/moisture exposure.

## 27. Priority 4 — Extend Proven Controls Where Applicable
After successful pilot/verification:
- survey other discharges for cleaner/containment applicability;

- confirm trip/restart material-handling procedure;
- confirm housekeeping/recovery responsibility and method;
- retain housekeeping as a supporting control while reducing recurrence through source correction.

# PART XII — CONCLUSION

## 29. Proposed Conclusion Direction
The audit indicates that spillage across the inspected Wessels material-handling system is not caused by one isolated defect. It is the visible result of several interacting failures of material and water containment occurring at different stages of the process.

Recurring fines carryback is the dominant system-wide mechanism, but significant coarse-product transit loss, local mistracking/alignment, water escape and event-driven unloading also contribute materially to the overall condition.

The evidence further demonstrates that visible accumulation cannot always be corrected effectively by acting only where the material is found. In many cases, the initiating mechanism is upstream or functionally separate from the final deposit location.

The recommended strategy is therefore to move from repeated consequence management toward **source control and process containment**:

- keep fines within the process until they reach the stage designed to handle them;
- keep coarse product within the carrying envelope until intended discharge;
- retain water within the screening/washing process;
- correct location-specific tracking, loading and structural issues;
- use housekeeping and operating procedures to support, rather than substitute for, engineering source control.

CV09 provides the preferred starting point for proving a cleaner/containment philosophy that can later be adapted to other suitable locations. W10, the Sample Conveyors, U4, Screens and L1 require targeted local actions because their mechanisms and geometry differ.

The overall goal is not simply a cleaner plant floor. It is a material-handling system in which the product remains controlled through the process and where unavoidable losses are contained, recoverable and managed deliberately.

# Narrative Writing Rules for the Next Draft
1. **Explain first, prove second.** Introduce what the condition means before presenting the detailed evidence.
2. **Use evidence as case proof, not as the report's organising principle.**
3. **Do not give every asset equal narrative weight.** Some are systemic case studies, some local exceptions, some comparators/supporting evidence.
4. **Retain formal evidence IDs and qualifications.** Narrative style must not reduce technical defensibility.
5. **Use transitions deliberately:** "This matters because...", "Taken together...", "The significance of this condition is...", "This differs from...".
6. **Preserve uncertainty openly.** A persuasive audit does not need to pretend every mechanism is fully proven.
7. **Do not optimise for the shortest report.** Optimise for the clearest evidence-to-conclusion story.
